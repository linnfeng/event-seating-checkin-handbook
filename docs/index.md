# Event seating and check-in handbook

A guest usually sees a seating assignment as a single instruction: find a name, then find a table. For the event team, that instruction depends on several records agreeing with one another. The guest list needs to match the physical room, the welcome team needs to know which version it is using, and the service team needs to hear about changes that affect it. The process below is designed for a small team that may have both venue staff and volunteers. It works on paper as well as in a shared editor.

## Identify which decisions belong to whom

Write down who can approve a change to the room, who can move a guest, and who maintains the live list. Those can be three different people. For instance, a host may know which relatives want to sit together, while the venue lead knows whether a chair may be added. The person greeting arrivals should not have to make either decision without a clear delegation. Give that person the name of a contact, a way to reach them, and a place to direct the guest while a problem is resolved.

Treat capacity and access as venue decisions. Find out which layout the venue has approved, whether furniture can still move on the day, and which routes must remain clear. Make a note of restrictions beside the affected table rather than burying them in an email thread. A floor plan is not a substitute for a venue inspection or any applicable safety requirement. Record who confirmed the setup and when so that a late change can be checked against the right baseline.

## Establish a roster that can survive edits

Use one row per person and separate internal identifiers from the names that appear on the sign. This helps when two guests share a name, an invitation lists a preferred name, or a household sends a correction. If people have consented to public display of their names, preview the exact spelling and format that will appear. A clerk can distinguish duplicate names with a private lookup or a pre-agreed reference rather than publishing extra personal information.

Keep attendance, group membership, table assignment and any chair assignment in separate fields. A row that says “confirmed” is not necessarily seated; a row with a table number does not guarantee attendance. An explicit unassigned list helps staff see those differences. Before exporting, check that all confirmed guests appear exactly once, that all allocated tables exist and that no table exceeds the approved count. Resolve or document any exception before sending the list to another team.

## Make an internal layout and a public lookup

The internal layout shows physical landmarks and operating notes: which door guests will use, where a queue may form and how staff will approach tables. A welcome desk copy should answer a narrower question: what should this arriving person do next? Give it a clear revision, the guest lookup and an obvious reference to the table labels. The public display can be smaller still. It need not expose the complete operations plan or explain why a seat was chosen.

Print or preview the display at real size. Stand where the first person in line will stand and follow the directions from there. Check that a person approaching from the opposite side can still identify the main entrance and that two tables do not have easily confused signs. If alphabetized lists are split across boards, label the letter ranges at a height that can be noticed before a visitor joins a queue. Leave the venue with a way to look up replacements if a board goes missing.

## Handle changes without losing the original picture

Make a pair of rehearsal changes before doors open. First, take one fictional guest off a full table. Decide whether the resulting seat becomes available immediately or only after a host authorizes it. Then add a fictional attendee who was not on the confirmed list. Practice recording the new assignment and telling the service lead if the change affects a place setting or meal. The rehearsal should use the same permissions and copy that volunteers will actually have.

For a small event, record each change with the time, old assignment, new assignment and initials of the person who approved it. For a larger event, assign a single editor and let other teams report changes through a separate channel. A distributed group editing every copy can make contradictory corrections without realizing it. Mark superseded lists clearly and take printed copies out of circulation when practical. If wireless access is unreliable, have a current printed fallback and agree on where handwritten corrections are recorded.

## Separate access requests from public instructions

Ask guests what arrangement would help them participate without requesting a diagnosis. A person may need to avoid a step, reach an aisle easily, face a speaker, or sit near someone who provides assistance. Confirm the route and facility with the venue before promising a specific seat. A chart may show a generous gap that is narrowed in practice by a lectern, bags or furniture being used by another activity. Check the setup after those items are in place.

Keep personal reasons off display materials. Pass the minimum necessary instructions to the people implementing them. For example, a room team needs to know that a clear approach should be maintained, not the medical information behind the request. After the event, keep general notes about the venue separately from the personal roster, and dispose of copies that are no longer needed under your retention practice.

## Do a final reconciliation at the venue

Have someone who did not draw the plan walk a guest route from the door to two representative tables. Ask them to read the sign aloud and locate a table without additional clues. Then test the same path while a few volunteers pause at the welcome desk. This can reveal a problem that a diagram hides, such as needing to cross a queue to get back to the seating display. Move the display or change directions only after confirming the new placement with the venue.

Before opening, reconcile the confirmed count, the unassigned list and the number of actual places. Keep a note of who has the current version. After arrivals settle, ask the welcome team which question it had to answer repeatedly; that is a good place to improve the next draft. A useful post-event summary records what worked in the room without keeping unnecessary details about individual guests.

## Practice the opening handoff

About fifteen minutes before opening, give each volunteer a copy marked with its intended role. Ask the desk lead to state the revision out loud, then ask the room lead to confirm that the table signs match it. If the copies disagree, stop distributing them until the roster editor resolves the mismatch. Set aside a small batch of blank place cards and a way to print a replacement if the plan uses assigned seats. Those supplies do not replace approval for a change; they let the team act promptly once a decision is made.
